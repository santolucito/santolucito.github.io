I'll present this as its author. The one thing to remember: learn the search. A neural network proposes programs in a FlashFill-style DSL, and executing them checks whether they're consistent. Because the proposer is learned, it can be trained to shrug off typos. The table is Figure 1 of the paper. Two of the example outputs have typos, so FlashFill's exact-match intersection would come back empty. The program the paper shows is GetToken(Alpha, -1) | ',' | ' ' | ToCase(Proper, GetToken(Alpha, 1)).

The problem

Two ways to learn string programs, each lacking

Rule-based (FlashFill)

Hand-crafted operator semantics, pruning and ranking. Accurate on clean examples.

But: “difficult to extend and fragile to noise”. Its efficiency rests on exact string matching.

Neural (before this paper)

Learned from data, so in principle extensible and tolerant of noise.

But: the best prior model (Parisotto et al., 2017) solved only 34% of real FlashFill tasks.

RobustFill · Devlin et al., ICML 2017

2

This is the gap we set out to close. Rule-based PBE systems like FlashFill work, but they're hand-engineered: each operator needs its inverse semantics written by hand, and exact matching means a typo breaks them. "Difficult to extend and fragile to noise" is our phrase from the introduction. The 34% figure is Parisotto et al.'s R3NN at beam 1000, from our Section 5.1 table. We also wanted the first head-to-head comparison of neural synthesis (generate a program) with neural induction (generate the output directly) on a real test set.

This picks up from the FlashFill talk. FlashFill went from a POPL paper to Excel within two years, and FlashMeta turned its witness-function and version-space machinery into a framework for any DSL. RobustFill keeps a very similar DSL but replaces the symbolic search with a sequence model, partly to handle noisy examples, which is exactly where version-space intersection is brittle. The test set, FlashFillTest, came from the FlashFill authors.

The one idea to remember

Learn the search. Let execution check it.

A network trained on hundreds of millions of random programs proposes a beam of candidates. Running each one on the examples keeps only the consistent ones. Noise becomes something to train on, not something that empties the search.

This is the slide to remember. FlashFill computes the exact set of consistent programs and then ranks them. We don't compute that set at all. A model proposes likely programs, and the DSL's deterministic interpreter filters them. That split, a neural proposer with a symbolic checker, is what makes this a neurosymbolic paper rather than a purely neural one. The training numbers are from Section 4.4: 2 million minibatches of 128 programs, so 256 million random programs.

5

This is the contrast to keep in mind for the rest of the talk. Both systems solve the same PBE problem over a similar DSL. FlashFill's guarantees come from exact reasoning, and so does its brittleness. We give up the guarantee that every consistent program was considered, but we get robustness and extensibility: to add an operator, you add it to the interpreter and the generator, and you don't write its inverse. FlashFill isn't defenceless against noise. Its Section 5.2 says that when the classifier fails, it drops a singleton partition, treats that example as a likely typo and asks the user (the "Kimberley" vs "Kimberly" example). That's a heuristic for one bad example, though, not robustness learned from data. Our noise handling is Section 7: train with synthetic character edits, and select the best program by character edit rate instead of exact match.

Figure 2 gives the syntax. Types t are Number, Word, Alphanum, AllCaps, PropCase, Lower, Digit and Char. Positions k run from -100 to 100, and indices i from -5 to 5, with negatives counting from the right. GetSpan returns the text between the i1-th match of r1 and the i2-th match of r2, and y picks the start or end of each match. From Section 3.2: there are about 30 million unique expressions, and search without inverse semantics can't prune partial expressions, so even dynamic programming would face millions of candidates. The 20% and 10× figures are from Section 5.1 and its footnote 6. Unlike FlashFill's DSL, there's no Switch or Loop: a program is a single concatenation.

Students often mix these up, so say it plainly: RobustFill is a program synthesizer. The neural network is a proposal mechanism. Beam search over it yields candidate programs in the DSL, and each candidate is an ordinary deterministic program that a normal interpreter runs. That's how RobustFill checks candidates against the observed examples, and how the final answer can be read and reused. The randomness lives only in the proposer, never in the program. The example program is the one in Figure 1 of the paper. Induction is the road not taken. The same kind of network is trained to emit the output string directly, one character at a time, with the program left implicit in its weights. The paper builds it only to compare (Section 6): 81% for synthesis vs. 53% for induction on all-examples-correct, although induction gets partial credit more often. Evaluation for both: a test task shows 4 examples and holds out 6, and it counts only if all 6 held-out outputs are exactly right.

RobustFill · Devlin et al., ICML 2017 · Section 4

8

The input is an unordered, variable-size set of I/O pairs, not one sequence. Late pooling is our answer. Each example gets its own I, O and P layers with shared weights, and P's hidden states are max-pooled across examples at every decoding step, before a single softmax. Parisotto et al. pooled at the encoder instead, which rules out attention. Strings are encoded at the character level over the 95 printable ASCII characters. Programs are linearized over a 430-token vocabulary. Layers have 512 units and embeddings 128. Training ran 2 million minibatches of 128 programs, about 24 hours on 2 Titan X GPUs (Section 4.4).

→

Keep the highest-scoring consistent one

DP-Beam

Each time an expression is completed, execute the partial program. If its output isn't a prefix of the target output, drop it from the beam. This works because the DSL is concatenative.

~0.3 s

per task on a Titan X GPU (Attention-C-DP, beam 100: 89%)

RobustFill · Devlin et al., ICML 2017 · Section 5

9

Unlike machine translation, we don't just take the 1-best output. We execute every candidate in the beam and take the highest-scoring one that is consistent with all observed examples. Footnote 5 says alternatives like "take the shortest program" didn't do better. DP-Beam adds roughly 5 points of accuracy. It's a lightweight symbolic check inside the search, in the same spirit as FlashFill's exact matching, so there's a bit of FlashFill living inside RobustFill. The 0.3 s figure is amortized end-to-end decoding cost with an unoptimized decoder.

The table is verbatim from Section 5.1. The metric is generalization accuracy: all six held-out outputs exactly right. We attribute the jump over Parisotto et al. to two things. Late pooling lets us use attention, and the DSL is more expressive, with GetSpan needed for about 20% of tasks. From Section 5: all the attentional variants beat the basic model by roughly 25 points absolute, and B and C each add roughly 2 to 5 more. The consistency analysis (Figure 5) shows that beam 1 finds a consistent program only about 50% of the time, so the learned semantics are "still far from perfect", and search is doing real work.

RobustFill · Devlin et al., ICML 2017 · Section 6

11

This is the first direct comparison of synthesis and induction on a real-world set, and it's a contribution in its own right. The induction model is Attention-A with late pooling, except that it decodes the output string and adds an encoder for the new input. It uses beam 3 and doesn't improve with a larger beam, because there's no way to check candidates. On all-example accuracy it's 53% vs 81% (Section 6.1). On average-example accuracy, synthesis still leads by about 10 points absolute, but induction gets partial credit far more often (Section 6.2). Our point is that the right choice depends on the product.

Noise = random character insertions, deletions and substitutions in the observed examples. RobustFill trains on the same kind of noise and picks the program with the lowest edit distance to the examples.

RobustFill · Devlin et al., ICML 2017 · Section 1 and Section 7

12

This is the headline result. The 80% vs 6% comparison is from our contributions list in Section 1. Section 7 and Figure 8 show the curve: the neural models degrade by about 2 points absolute per noise character, while Excel FlashFill is "effectively broken" with just one or two characters of noise. That's expected, because its algorithm is built around exact string matching. Its only noise defence is a heuristic that flags one example it can't classify as a likely typo (FlashFill Section 5.2), and that doesn't cover typos scattered across several examples. Only two changes were needed: inject noise into the training data (this didn't hurt clean accuracy, footnote 9), and select P* by character edit rate instead of exact match. The Excel numbers come from a macro in Excel 2016. Be upfront about footnote 11: FlashFill was manually developed on this exact test set, so its clean 92% is on its home turf.

Champion · the case for a best paper award

Why this paper matters beyond its benchmark

Neural synthesis that works

92% on 205 real spreadsheet tasks, up from 34% for the best earlier neural model, and on par with FlashFill on clean data.

No hand-written inverses

To add an operator, add it to the interpreter and the random generator. Typos become something you train on: 80% vs 6% with noise.

What I'd build on it

The template of a neural proposer with a symbolic checker. Next week, DeepCoder uses a network to guide a symbolic search instead of replacing it.

Conceded up front: the training programs and the noise are synthetic

Both come from generators the authors wrote, and the noise at test time has the same form as in training. That doesn't sink it: the headline accuracy is measured on 205 real tasks, and the core claim, that a learned proposer checked by execution rivals a hand-built synthesizer, is tested there.

RobustFill · Devlin et al., ICML 2017

13

The Champion argues for a best paper award, specific rather than enthusiastic, and concedes the biggest weakness first. The three cards: the result (92% vs 34%, Section 5.1), extensibility and noise (80% vs 6%, Sections 1 and 7), and the design pattern that later work builds on. The concession is the synthetic data and noise; the defence is that the test set is real (FlashFillTest, Section 3.3). Be ready for the Critic's comeback: footnote 11 says FlashFill was developed on this same test set, which cuts both ways.

The Critic's slide: attack the claims, not the authors, and close with one experiment. As the author, my answers: training on random data works because the model learns function semantics, not a data distribution (our argument in Section 3.3). Beam search plus execution is part of the method, not a crutch. The fair points: noise at test time has the same form as noise at training time, and the FlashFill baseline's only noise handling is its single-typo heuristic (FlashFill Section 5.2), not a symbolic system designed for noise. Also note the compute asymmetry: about 24 hours of training on two GPUs versus a hand-built CPU system.

Discussion

What would we have to believe?

1

RobustFill still needs the DSL to generate its training data and to check its answers. Is it neural, or neurosymbolic?

2

Synthesis wins on all-or-nothing accuracy; induction earns partial credit. Which matches what users want?

3

Could one system have FlashFill's guarantees and RobustFill's tolerance for typos? What would it look like?

These questions link the two papers of the week. For question 1, point out that the symbolic parts (the interpreter, DP-Beam pruning and the random program generator) do a lot of work. For question 3, possible directions include neural proposals checked by version spaces, version spaces over edit-tolerant matching, and learned ranking (as in later PROSE work). This also sets up next week's DeepCoder, where the network guides a symbolic search instead of replacing it.
